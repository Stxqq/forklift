Forklift

Small forklift robots work a warehouse. They plan the quickest way around each other, lift a package out of its bay, read the QR label and drop it at the right dock, and a delivery log checks every step against the order. The scan is real: the label is decoded from pixels, smudges and all. Watch one, dispatch a few, or drive one.

    How it works

    Every 1/60 s each robot moves one fixed step. Orders go to whichever free robot gets to the bay soonest, and every job runs through the same five stages.

    1. 01An order names a package, the bay the records say it's in, and a dock. In Watch they come from the stock records; in Dispatch from your clicks.
    2. 02Space-time A* plans the quickest route in seconds, not meters: corners are slower arcs, starting off costs time, and every other robot's plan is booked in a reservation table, so it can wait a moment or take a detour, whichever is sooner.
    3. 03Roads have a lane each way and robots keep right. A robot only drives into cells it holds, and takes a crossing together with the cell after it, so nobody stops in the middle of one.
    4. 04The fork goes up to the shelf on the way in. At the bay the robot turns, reaches in and lifts. An empty bay is reported missing.
    5. 05The scanner renders what it sees as pixels, finds the code and decodes it, Reed–Solomon and all. A wrong label goes back on the shelf; two failed reads flag the bay for a person.

    Who checks what

    The delivery log doesn't trust the robot. Each line is worked out from what the sensors recorded: what the fork found, what the scanner decoded and what was set down at which dock, against what the order asked for. About one box in ten sits in the wrong bay or isn't there at all, and one in five has a smudged or torn label, so the log has something to catch.
